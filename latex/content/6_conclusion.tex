\section{Summary and Conclusions}

Over the course of computation, computer programs use memory to record intermediate results. Similarly, residual vectors within a deep neural network must encode intermediate ``features'' relevant to the task being carried out. Reverse engineering representations used by large neural networks is a broad goal considered within mechanistic interpretability. (See, for example, Section 2.1 of \cite{sharkey_open_2025}.)

A common assumption, stemming from the point of view of connectionism, is that these vectors can be viewed as distributed representations. Roughly, this means that different underlying features $x_i$ are simultaneously represented by patterns of activity taking place over the whole vector $y$. Unfortunately, inferring such a representation can be a difficult statistical problem. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) have been proposed as a solution.

Given a model for a distributed representation, consider the problem of inferring the latent features $x_i$ from the vector $y$. One possible approach is to estimate each latent feature \textit{independently}, modeling the contribution of the remaining features as some kind of noise. This essentially coincides with the approach used by most SAEs. Of course, it may be possible to do better by an iterative approach, where the running estimate for one variable $x_j$ can inform the estimation of another variable $x_j$. What is not obvious a priori is \textit{how much better} we can expect an iterative approach to be. Are iterative estimates necessary to decode distributed representations?

This work has provided some mathematical observations that may help to answer this question.

The most obvious result of our findings is to suggest that the performance of SAEs may be improved by using slightly more computation to solve the sparse reconstruction problem.\footnote{Note that the improved ``encoder layers'' would need to be used during training of an SAE; if sparse reconstruction is solved inaccurately while the dictionary is being optimized, it is natural that many codewords will not be inferred.} However, we hope that the information-theoretic point of view described in this work is enlightening.
