\section{Summary and Conclusions}

Over the course of computation, computer programs use memory to record intermediate results. Similarly, residual vectors within a deep neural network must encode intermediate ``features'' relevant to the task being carried out. Reverse engineering representations used by large neural networks is a broad goal considered within mechanistic interpretability. (See, for example, Section 2.1 of \cite{sharkey_open_2025}.)

A common assumption, familiar from the point of view of connectionism, is that these vectors should be modeled as distributed representations. Roughly, this means that different underlying features $x_i$ are simultaneously represented by patterns of activity taking place over a whole vector $y$. Unfortunately, inferring such a representation can be a difficult statistical problem.

Assuming an expression $y$ in terms of a vector $x$ of latent features, consider the subproblem of inferring $x$ in terms of $y$. One possible approach is to estimate each latent feature independently, approximating the contribution of the remaining features via a noise term. This essentially coincides with the approach used by most sparse autoencoders. Of course, it may be possible to do better by an iterative approach, where the running estimate for one factor $x_j$ can improve our estimate of another factor $x_i$. What is not obvious a priori is \textit{how much better} we can expect an iterative approach to be. Are iterative estimates necessary to decode distributed representations?

This work studies basic mathematical phenomena that may help answer this question. In a specific toy scenario, the sample complexity of ``one-step methods'' is a constant factor of the Shannon limit so long as the feature vector $x$ is extremely sparse. More specifically, we showed that number of dimensions required to transmit each bit of information can be predicted as a function of the parameter $\eta = \ln k / \ln N.$ On the other hand, we showed experimentally that even a small constant factor of addtional computation can reduce sample complexity significantly.

The most obvious result of our findings is to suggest that the performance of SAEs may be improved by using slightly more computation to solve the sparse reconstruction problem.\footnote{Note that the improved encoder layers would need to be used during the dictionary learning process; if sparse reconstruction is solved inaccurately during, it is natural that many codewords will not be inferred.} However, we hope that the information-theoretic point of view described in this work is enlightening.
