\section{Superposition Codes and Sparse Recovery \label{sec:codes}}

We begin by describing the toy scenario to be studied. Given a large number $N,$ consider a map $F$ that represents each subset $x \subseteq [N] = \left\{ 1, \dots, N \right\}$ by a linear combination
$$
	y(x) = \sum_{i \in x} f_i \in \R^d,
$$
where the vectors $\{ f_i \in \R^d : i \in [N] \}$ are chosen in advance and where the dimension $d$ of the encoding is expected to be much smaller than $N.$ The vectors $f_i$ are called codewords for the elements of $[N],$ the collection $\{ f_1, \dots, f_N \}$ is called a dictionary, and the image $Fx$ is called a superposition code. It is useful to view $x$ as a vector in $\left\{ 0, 1 \right\}^N$ with coefficients
$$
	x_i = \begin{cases}
		1: i \in x \\
		0 : \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
$$
and view $F$ the matrix of column vectors $[f_1 \; \dots \; f_N],$ so that $y = F x$ is a linear equation. In this work, we'll model our subset as a random variable $X$ uniformly distributed over all subsets of some fixed size $k \ll N.$ Keeping with the orders of magnitude discussed in \cite{gao_scaling_2024}, we consider $N \approx 1{\small,}000{\small,}000$ and $k \approx 100.$

The problem of recovering a sparse signal $x$ from a linear projection $F x$ is known as sparse recovery. This problem is considered in the subject of compressive sensing. In this work, we will use language from coding theory and think of $x$ as some data to be encoded and the projection $y = F x$ as a code. Thus, sparse recovery becomes the task of ``decoding'' $x.$ Note that this is opposite to the convention used in sparse autoencoders, where the map estimating $x$ from $y$ is called the ``encoder.'' (In classical applications of autoencoders, the learned representation is understood as an efficient code for the data distribution.)

Due to a connection with sparse linear regression, the subproblem of identifying the support of $x$ from the projection $F x$ is often called model selection. When the coordinates of $x$ take values in $\{0, 1 \},$ sparse recovery reduces to a problem of model selection. In general, note that model selection presents the main difficulty of sparse recovery; once the support of $x$ identified, its non-zero coordinates can be estimated easily e.g.\ by solving a least squares problem.

In practice, a dictionary $F$ employed by neural networks will likely have special properties related to the nature of the data being encoded and the operations that need to be performed. However, for the purposes of encoding a uniformly random subset of $[N],$ one natural way to choose a dictionary is to assign each codeword $f_i$ to a random unit-norm vector. The simplest motivation for this strategy is the fact that, so long as $d = \Omega(\ln N),$ we can expect a dictionary of $N$ randomly chosen vectors to be a ``nearly orthonormal'' frame in the following sense.

\begin{proposition}
	\label{prop:many-ortho}
	For $\epsilon > 0$ and $p > 0,$ let $d > 2 \epsilon^{-2} (2 \ln N + \ln p^{-1}),$ and let $\{ F_1, \dots, F_N \}$
	be independent draws from a uniform distribution over the unit sphere in $\R^N.$ Then
	\begin{equation*}
	\forall i \neq j, \; \lvert \langle F_i, F_j \rangle \rvert < \epsilon
	\end{equation*}
	with probability at least $(1 - p).$
\end{proposition}

From this result it can be seen that, for fixed $k,$ it suffices to take $d = \Omega(\ln N)$ in order for each coefficient $x_i$ to be retrievable directly by thresholding the linear function $\langle y, f_i \rangle$ of the code $y.$ However, we will see that the values of $N$ needed to reliably encode a random set $Y$ varies significantly depending on the method of decoding used.

We informally refer to decoding methods that require only a single ``step'' of estimation and thresholding as \textbf{one-step estimates}. In contrast, the field of compressive sensing provides a number of more elaborate \textit{iterative} methods for sparse recovery. However, many of these methods are not applicable to the very large problems faced by sparse autoencoders, where performing a training run using only a one-step method is already computationally demanding. In this work, we consider a family of relatively cheap iterative algorithms which we call \textbf{matching-$k$ methods}.

\subsection{One-Step Methods}

One way to motivate the construction of one-step estimates is to think of the isolated subproblem of estimating a coefficient $X_i$ from the model
\begin{equation}
	Y = X_i f_i + \overbrace{\sum_{j \neq i} X_j f_j}^{Z_i}. \label{eq:simple-model}
\end{equation}
(Here we write $X$ and $Y$ with capital letters to emphasize they are random variables.) If we approximate the sum $Z_i$ of other codewords as centered Gaussian noise with covariance $\Sigma,$ the inference problem for $X_i$ becomes tractable; specifically, if we define an inner product by $\langle v, w \rangle_\Sigma = \frac 1 2 v^T \Sigma^{-1} w,$ then the posterior on $X_i$ can be expressed in terms of the linear function
$$
	\lambda_i(Y) = \frac{\langle f_i, Y \rangle_\Sigma}{\lVert f \rVert_\Sigma^2}.
$$
In signal processing, this is called a matched filter for the codeword $f_i.$ In terms of the matched filter,
\begin{align} \label{eq:log_odds}
	 \ln \Pp(X_i = x|Y = y)  = \frac {\rho_i} 2 \left(x - \lambda_i(y) \right)^2 + \ln \Pp(X_i = x) + C
\end{align}
where $\rho_i = (\Var \lambda_i(Z_i))^{-1}$ is called the ``signal-to-noise ratio.'' (See \Cref{appendix:matched} for a review.)

When $f_i$ are independent draws from some isotropic distribution, the covariance $\Sigma$ can be modeled as a multiple of the identity. In this case we have $\lambda_i(Y) = \langle f_i, Y \rangle / \lVert f_i \rVert,$ and the maximum a posteriori estimate is for $X_i$ is simply
$$
\hat x_i(y) = \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle 1 : \frac{\langle f_i, y \rangle}{\lVert f_i \rVert} \ge \frac 1 2 + \rho_i^{-1} \ln \frac{\Pp(X_i = 1)}{\Pp(X_i = 0)}
    \\
    0 : \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$
where we recall that $\rho_i^{-1} = \Var \, \lambda_i(Z_i).$ This last quantity can be estimated by computing the variance under a random dictionary $F$; since $\Var\, \langle F_i, F_j \rangle = 1/d$ for independent vectors uniformly distributed on the unit sphere in $\R^d,$ we have
\begin{align*}
\Var_{F, X} \, \langle F_i, Z_i \rangle & =
\Pp(X_i = 1) \Var(\langle F_i, Z_i \rangle | X_i = 1) + \Pp(X_i = 0) \Var(\langle F_i, Z_i \rangle | X_i = 1)  \\
    & = \frac{k(k - 1) + (N - k)k}{Nd} = \frac{k - k/N} {d} \approx \frac k d
\end{align*}

Now, if the number $k$ of non-zero latents is known or can be guessed in advance, one simple improvement is to perform thresholding on the basis of this information---that is, to set $\hat x_i(y) = 1$ for the $k$ indices $i$ where the filters $\lambda_i(y)$ are maximized. We call this the top-$k$ method.

and it the natural analog of the usual top-$k$ thresholding operation \cite{makhzani_k-sparse_2014}. \cite{gao_scaling_2024} showed that, in practice, top-$k$ autoencoders perform better than their ReLU variants in practice.
