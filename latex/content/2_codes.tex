\section{Superposition Codes and Sparse Recovery \label{sec:codes}}

We begin by describing the toy scenario to be studied. Given a large number $N,$ consider a map $F$ that represents each subset $x \subseteq [N] = \left\{ 1, \dots, N \right\}$ by a linear combination
$$
	y(x) = \sum_{i \in x} f_i \in \R^d,
$$
where the vectors $\{ f_i \in \R^d : i \in [N] \}$ are chosen in advance and where the dimension $d$ of the encoding is expected to be much smaller than $N.$ The vectors $f_i$ are called codewords for the elements of $[N],$ the collection $\{ f_1, \dots, f_N \}$ is called a dictionary, and the image $Fx$ is called a superposition code. By viewing $x$ as a vector in $\left\{ 0, 1 \right\}^N$ with coefficients
$$
	x_i = \begin{cases}
		1: i \in x \\
		0 : \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
$$
and viewing $F$ as the matrix of column vectors $[f_1 \; \dots \; f_N],$ we can write $y = F x$ as a linear equation. In this work, we'll model our subset as a random variable $X$ uniformly distributed over all subsets of some fixed size $k \ll N.$ In keeping with the orders of magnitude discussed in \citet{gao_scaling_2024}, we consider $N \approx 2^{20}$ and $k \approx 64$ as reference values.

In general, the problem of recovering a sparse signal $x$ from a linear projection $F x$ is known as sparse recovery. In this work, we will use language from coding theory and think of $x$ as some data to be encoded and the projection $y = F x$ as a code. Thus, sparse recovery becomes the task of ``decoding'' $x.$ Note that this is opposite to the convention used in sparse autoencoders, where the map estimating $x$ from $y$ is called the ``encoder.'' (In classical applications of autoencoders, the learned representation is typically understood as an efficient code for the data distribution and has fewer dimensions than the data being modeled.)

Due to a connection with sparse linear regression, the problem of identifying the support of a vector $x$ from a linear projection $F x$ is often called model selection. When the coordinates of $x$ take values in $\{0, 1 \},$ sparse recovery and model selection coincide. In general, note that model selection presents the main difficulty of sparse recovery; once the support of $x$ is identified, its non-zero coordinates can be estimated easily, e.g.\ by solving a least squares problem.

In practice, a dictionary $F$ employed by a neural network will likely have special properties related to the nature of the data being encoded and the operations that need to be performed. However, for the purposes of encoding a uniformly random subset of $[N],$ one natural way to choose a dictionary is to make each codeword an independent random vector. The simplest motivation for this strategy is the fact that, so long as $d = \Omega(\ln N),$ a dictionary of $N$ randomly chosen vectors is highly ``incoherent.'' For example, we have the following.

\begin{proposition}
	\label{prop:many-ortho}
	For $\mu > 0$ and $p > 0,$ let $d > 2 \mu^{-2} (2 \ln N + \ln p^{-1}),$ and let $\{ F_1, \dots, F_N \}$ be independent draws from $\{-1/\sqrt d, 1/\sqrt d\}^d.$ Then
	\begin{equation}
	\forall i \neq j, \; \lvert \langle F_i, F_j \rangle \rvert < \mu \label{eq:incoherence}
	\end{equation}
	with probability at least $(1 - p).$
\end{proposition}

For a standard proof, see \Cref{appendix:basic}.

The infimum of all constants $\mu$ satisfying the condition \eqref{eq:incoherence} is called the mutual coherence of a dictionary. Bounds on mutual coherence give a relatively simple way to guarantee the success of various sparse recovery algorithms; for example, see Chapter 4 of \citet{elad_sparse_2010}. In our scenario, we can say the following.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:threshold}
	Given a dictionary $F = \{ F_1, \dots, F_N \} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ of unit norm codewords with mutual coherence smaller than $1/2k,$ for any $k$-sparse vector $x \in \{0,1\}^N$ and for all $i = 1, \dots, N$ we have
	$$
	\forall i, x_i = \begin{cases}
 	  1 : \langle F_i, F x \rangle \ge 1/2 \\
 	  0 : \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
	$$
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose that $x_i = 0.$ By assumption that the mutual coherence of $F$ is smaller than $1/2k,$ we have $\langle F_i, F_j \rangle < 1/2k$ for all $i \neq j,$ and so
$$
\langle F_i, F x \rangle = \sum_{x_j = 1} \langle F_i, F_j \rangle < \frac k {2 k} = \frac 1 2.
$$
Similarly, using that $\langle F_i, F_i \rangle = 1$ and $\langle F_i, F_j \rangle > -1/2k$ for all $i \neq j,$ if $x_i = 1$ we find that
$$
\langle F_i, F x \rangle = \langle F_i, F_i \rangle + \sum_{x_j = 1 \land j \neq i} \langle F_i, F_j \rangle \ge 1 - \frac{(k - 1)}{2k} \ge 1/2.
$$
\end{proof}

Overall we can already guarantee that, for a fixed value of $k,$ the dimension $d$ of our superposition code need only grow logarithmically with respect to the size $N$ of the dictionary for a very simple method to succeed at recovering $x$ from $F x.$

\begin{corollary}
	Let $k \ge 1$ and $N \ge 1$ be any integers and $p > 0,$ and let
	$$
	d \ge 4 k^2 (2 \ln N + \ln p^{-1}).
	$$
	Then given a dictionary $F = \{ F_1, \dots, F_k \}$ in the conditions of \Cref{prop:many-ortho}, it is true with probability at least $(1 - p)$ that every $k$-sparse vector $x \in \R^N$ can be recovered from $F x$ by the formula
	$$
	x_i = \begin{cases}
 	  1 : \langle f_i, F x \rangle \ge 1/2 \\
 	  0 : \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
	$$
\end{corollary}

In the remainder of this work, we are interested in refining this prediction and understanding how sample complexity (the minimum required value of $d$) depends on the method we use to infer $x.$ For simplicity we will consider only Rademacher dictionaries in our theoretical results, although in our numerical experiments (\Cref{sec:numerical}) we show that dictionaries with codewords drawn uniformly from the unit sphere behave very similarly.

Finally, it remains to define the decoding methods we will study. We informally refer to decoding methods that require only a single ``step'' of estimation and thresholding, like the estimate in \Cref{prop:threshold}, as one-step decoders. In this work, we will consider two one-step methods and one very simple iterative algorithm which we call generalized matching pursuit (GMP).

\subsection{Threshold Decoding and Top-k Decoding \label{sec:threshold-top-k}}

To motivate one-step decoders, consider the problem of estimating a single coefficient $X_i$ from the model
\begin{equation}
	Y = X_i f_i + \overbrace{\sum_{j \neq i} X_j f_j}^{Z_i}. \label{eq:simple-model}
\end{equation}
(Throughout, our convention be to write random variables with capital letters.) If we model the sum $Z_i$ as centered Gaussian noise with covariance $\Sigma,$ the inference problem for $X_i$ becomes tractable; specifically, if we define an inner product by $\langle v, w \rangle_\Sigma = \frac 1 2 v^T \Sigma^{-1} w,$ then the maximum likelihood estimator for $X_i$ is the linear function
$$
	\lambda_i(Y) = \frac{\langle f_i, Y \rangle_\Sigma}{\lVert f \rVert_\Sigma^2}.
$$
In signal processing, estimating a scalar in the presence of noise is called filtering. In the context of a Gaussian model, estimates like $\lambda_i(Y)$ are called matched filters. In terms of our matched filter, we can express the exact posterior as
\begin{align} \label{eq:log_odds}
	 \ln \Pp(X_i = x|Y = y)  = -\frac {\rho_i} 2 \left(x - \lambda_i(y) \right)^2 + \ln \Pp(X_i = x) + C
\end{align}
where $\rho_i = (\Var \lambda_i(Z_i))^{-1}$ is called the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). (See \Cref{appendix:matched} for a review.) When $F$ is a random dictionary of unit-norm codewords, we can model $Z_i$ as an isotropic Gaussian, in which case our matched filters take the form $\lambda_i(Y) = \langle f_i, Y \rangle.$

One simple decoding strategy would be to base our estimates $\hat X_i(Y) \approx X_i$ directly on the matched filters $\langle f_i, Y \rangle.$ Specifically, we may put $\hat X_i = 1$ if $\langle f_i, Y \rangle$ is larger than some threshold $\tau$ and $\hat X_i = 0$ otherwise. With $\tau = 1/2,$ this corresponds to the formula we considered earlier in \Cref{prop:threshold}. We call this method \textbf{threshold decoding} at level $\tau$ (Algorithm \ref{alg:threshold}).

Substituting the prior
$$
\Pp(X_i = 0) = \frac{N - k}{N}, \quad \Pp(X_i = 1) = \frac k N
$$
into our expression \eqref{eq:log_odds} for the posterior of the Gaussian model shows that the maximum a posteriori estimate for $X_i$ is equivalent to thresholding at level
\begin{equation}
\tau = \frac 1 2 - \rho_i^{-1} \ln \frac{\Pp(X_i = 1)}{\Pp(X_i = 0)} = \frac 1 2 - \rho_i^{-1} \ln \frac k {N - k}. \label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}

Using the fact that $\Var\, \langle F_i, F_j \rangle = 1/d$ for a pair of independent codewords drawn from either a Rademacher or spherical dictionary, it's easy to show that $\Var \, \langle f_i, Y \rangle = (N - 1)k/Nd.$ Plugging this expression in as the variance $\Var \, \lambda_i(Z_i)$ in our fictitious Gaussian model gives the thresholding level
\begin{align*}
\tau & = \frac 1 2 - \frac {N - 1} N \frac k d \ln \frac{k}{N - k} \\
    & = \frac 1 2 - \frac{N - 1}N \frac k d \left(\ln \frac k N + \frac k N + O \left( \frac{k^2}{N^2} \right)\right).
\end{align*}
Since we expect $N \gg k,$ it is reasonable to drop terms of order $O(k/N).$ This lets us simplify the expression for the optimal threshold to
$$
\tau_\text{MAP} = \frac 1 2 + \left(1 - \frac{\ln k}{\ln N} \right) \frac{k \ln N}{d}.
$$
We refer to threshold decoding at level $\tau_\text{MAP}$ as \textbf{MAP decoding}.

Another very simple strategy is to first compute all the matched filters $F^T Y$ and find which are largest. We will call this top-$k$ decoding (Algorithm \ref{alg:top-k}). Top-$k$ has the disadvantage that $k$ must be known exactly in advance, whereas threshold decoding only requires $k$ to adjust the threshold $\tau.$ However, should $k$ be known, it is clear that top-$k$ is strictly more reliable than threshold decoding for any $\tau.$

\setlength{\algomargin}{1.5em}
\begin{figure}[h]
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\textwidth}
\begin{algorithm}[H] \label{alg:threshold}
\KwIn{$y \in \R^d$, $F \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N}$, $\tau \in \R$}
\KwOut{$\hat{x} \in \{0,1\}^N$}
$\hat x \gets 0$ \;
$\hat x \gets 1 \text{ where } F^T y \ge \tau$ \;
\Return $\hat x$
\caption{Threshold decoding}
\end{algorithm}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\textwidth}
\begin{algorithm}[H] \label{alg:top-k}
\KwIn{$y \in \mathbb{R}^d$, $F \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N}$, $k \in \N$}
\KwOut{$\hat{x} \in \{0,1\}^N$}
$\hat x \gets 0$ \;
$\hat x \gets 1 \text{ at $k$ largest values of } F^T y$ \;
\Return $\hat{x}$
\caption{Top-$k$ decoding}
\end{algorithm}
\end{minipage}
\end{figure}

 In the case that $F^T y$ is maximized at some set $\hat X$ containing more than $k$ indices, top-$k$ is understood to choose $k$ of these indices using a (potentially non-deterministic) function of $\hat X$.

Note that in some works \citep{elad_sparse_2010} top-$k$ is known as the thresholding algorithm. Finally, note that in practice the latent quantities $X_i$ to be estimated take values besides $0$ and $1.$ The two algorithms described here can be viewed as simplifications of one-step methods currently used by autoencoders. Sparse autoencoders using top-$k$ activations were first suggested by \citet{makhzani_k-sparse_2014}.

\subsection{Generalized Matching Pursuit \label{sec:gmp}}

As discussed earlier, the field of compressive sensing provides various iterative algorithms for sparse recovery. For example, one classic starting point is to apply proximal gradient descent to the objective
$$
\frac {1}{2 d} \lVert F x - y \rVert_2^2 + \frac{\lambda}{d} \lVert x \rVert_1
$$
where the parameter $\lambda > 0$ can be understood as a Lagrange multiplier. This gives the iterative soft thresholding algorithm (ISTA) \citep{daubechies_iterative_2003}, and is the basis for methods like FISTA \citep{beck_fast_2009}. A single iteration of ISTA involves a matrix-vector product followed by a thresholding operation, and so is computationally comparable to top-$k$ or MAP thresholding. Unrolling ISTA and optimizing its parameters, as we would do for a deep neural network, is called learned ISTA (LISTA) \citep{gregor_learning_2010}. Another notable family of methods, which are not derived from a linear programming objective but instead from a message passing formulation, are based on the approximate message passing algorithm \citep{maleki_approximate_2010}. However, for sparse autoencoders, a training run using top-$k$ is already very computationally expensive. Therefore, it is interesting to study the properties of relatively cheap modifications to top-$k$.

In this work, we suggest a simple iterative generalization of top-$k$, which we call \textbf{generalized matching pursuit} (GMP). Rather than finding the $k$ codewords that are most correlated with the superposition $y$ all at once, GMP begins by finding the $k_1 < k$ most correlated codewords and proceeds iteratively, subtracting the selected codewords from the value of $y$ supplied to the next iteration. We fix the number $T$ of iterations in advance and choose the numbers $(k_1, \dots, k_T)$ of codewords to be selected at each iteration as evenly as possible subject to the constraint $k_1 + \dots + k_T = k.$

\setlength{\algomargin}{1.5em}
\begin{algorithm}[h!]
\KwIn{Code $y \in \mathbb{R}^d$, dictionary $F \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N}$, sparsity $k$, steps $T$}
\KwOut{Latent $\hat{x} \in \{0,1\}^N$}
Choose any step sizes $k_1, \ldots, k_T$ so that $\sum_t k_t = k$ and $\max_t k_t$ is minimized \;
$\hat{x} \gets \mathbf{0}$ \;
\For{$t = 1, \dots, T$}{
    $\hat{x} \gets 1 \text{ at $k_t$ largest values of }
    F^T (y - F \hat x)$ \;
}
\Return $\hat{x}$
\caption{Generalized matching pursuit (GMP) with $T$ steps} \label{alg:gmp}
\end{algorithm}

Since the most computationally expensive step of Algorithm \ref{alg:gmp} is to compute the product $F^T (y - F \hat x),$ while the vector $r = y - F \hat x$ can be efficiently updated from one iteration to the next, GMP with $T$ steps has roughly $T$ times the computational requirements of top-$k$. Furthermore, we will find in practice that only $T = 3$ iterations is enough to significantly improve over top-$k$, even as the size of the inference problem varies over orders of magnitude.

The idea of GMP is closely related to well-known techniques in compressive sensing. For instance, the well-known matching pursuit \citep{mallat_matching_1993} and orthogonal matching pursuit \citep{tropp_signal_2007} algorithms infer a sparse signal $x$ by greedily selecting, one at a time, the column of $F$ most correlated with the current residual. (Matching pursuit subtracts the chosen codeword's contribution from the residual, while orthogonal matching pursuit re-fits the residual against all selected codewords at each step.) The technique of selecting multiple codewords at once was also proposed in \citep{wang_generalized_2012}, as an extension to orthogonal matching pursuit. In this context, GMP can be understood as a simplification of generalized orthogonal matching pursuit adapted to the case where the coordinates of $x$ are known to belong to $\{0, 1\}.$
