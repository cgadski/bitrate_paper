\section{Superposition Codes and Sparse Recovery \label{sec:codes}}

We begin by describing the toy scenario to be studied. Given a large number $N,$ consider a map $F$ that represents each subset $x \subseteq [N] = \left\{ 1, \dots, N \right\}$ by a linear combination
$$
	y(x) = \sum_{i \in x} f_i \in \R^d,
$$
where the vectors $\{ f_i \in \R^d : i \in [N] \}$ are chosen in advance and where the dimension $d$ of the encoding is expected to be much smaller than $N.$ The vectors $f_i$ are called codewords for the elements of $[N],$ the collection $\{ f_1, \dots, f_N \}$ is called a dictionary, and the image $Fx$ is called a superposition code. It is useful to view $x$ as a vector in $\left\{ 0, 1 \right\}^N$ with coefficients
$$
	x_i = \begin{cases}
		1: i \in x \\
		0 : \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
$$
and view $F$ the matrix of column vectors $[f_1 \; \dots \; f_N],$ so that $y = F x$ is a linear equation. In this work, we'll model our subset as a random variable $X$ uniformly distributed over all subsets of some fixed size $k \ll N.$ Keeping with the orders of magnitude discussed in \citet{gao_scaling_2024}, we consider $N \approx 2^{20}$ and $k \approx 64.$

The problem of recovering a sparse signal $x$ from a linear projection $F x$ is known as sparse recovery. This problem is considered in the subject of compressive sensing. In this work, we will use language from coding theory and think of $x$ as some data to be encoded and the projection $y = F x$ as a code. Thus, sparse recovery becomes the task of ``decoding'' $x.$ Note that this is opposite to the convention used in sparse autoencoders, where the map estimating $x$ from $y$ is called the ``encoder.'' (In classical applications of autoencoders, the learned representation typically is understood as an efficient code for the data distribution, and typically has smaller dimension than the variable being modeled.)

Due to a connection with sparse linear regression, the subproblem of identifying the support of $x$ from the projection $F x$ is often called model selection. When the coordinates of $x$ take values in $\{0, 1 \},$ sparse recovery reduces to a problem of model selection. In general, note that model selection presents the main difficulty of sparse recovery; once the support of $x$ identified, its non-zero coordinates can be estimated easily e.g.\ by solving a least squares problem.

In practice, a dictionary $F$ employed by a neural network will likely have special properties related to the nature of the data being encoded and the operations that need to be performed. However, for the purposes of encoding a uniformly random subset of $[N],$ one natural way to choose a dictionary is to make each codeword an independent random vector. The simplest motivation for this strategy is the fact that, so long as $d = \Omega(\ln N),$ a dictionary of $N$ randomly chosen vectors is highly ``incoherent.'' For example, we can prove the following for dictionaries of Rademacher vectors.

\begin{proposition}
	\label{prop:many-ortho}
	For $\mu > 0$ and $p > 0,$ let $d > 2 \mu^{-2} (2 \ln N + \ln p^{-1}),$ and let $\{ F_1, \dots, F_N \}$ be independent draws from $\{-1/\sqrt d, 1/\sqrt d\}^d.$ Then
	\begin{equation}
	\forall i \neq j, \; \lvert \langle F_i, F_j \rangle \rvert < \mu \label{eq:incoherence}
	\end{equation}
	with probability at least $(1 - p).$
\end{proposition}

For a standard proof, see \Cref{appendix:basic}.

The infimum of all constants $\mu$ satisfying the condition \eqref{eq:incoherence} is called the mutual coherence of a dictionary. Bounds on mutual coherence give a relatively simple way to guarantee the success of various sparse recovery algorithms; for example, see Chapter 4 of \citet{elad_sparse_2010}. In our scenario, we can say the following.

\begin{remark}\label{threshold-remark}
	Let $x \subseteq [N]$ be a $k$-sparse subset. Given a dictionary $F$ with mutual coherence smaller than $1/2k,$ for any $k$-sparse vector $x \in \R^N$ and for all $i = 1, \dots, N$ we have
	$$
	\forall i, x_i = \begin{cases}
 	  1 : \langle f_i, F x \rangle \ge 1/2 \\
 	  0 : \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
	$$
\end{remark}

Altogether we find that, for a fixed $k,$ it suffices to take $d = \Omega(\ln N)$ in order for each coefficient $x_i$ to be retrievable in a very simple way. In the remainder of this work, we are interested in refining this prediction, and especially in understanding how the minimum reliable value of $d$ depends on our decoding method.

We consider two natural constructions for the dictionary $F.$ A \textbf{Rademacher dictionary} is a random dictionary with codewords drawn as in \Cref{prop:many-ortho}, i.e.\ independent Rademacher vectors normalized to have unit norm. A \textbf{spherical dictionary} is a random dictionary with codewords drawn uniformly and independently from the unit sphere. These dictionaries have similar incoherence properties in our regime of interest; a result we prove for one dictionary (such as \Cref{prop:many-ortho}) can always be proven in an equivalent form for the other.

Finally, it remains to define the decoding methods we will study. We informally refer to decoding methods that require only a single ``step'' of estimation and thresholding---like the estimate in \Cref{threshold-remark}---as \textbf{one-step decoders}. In contrast, the field of compressive sensing provides a number of iterative methods for sparse recovery, which are known to perform more reliably in general. However, many of these methods are too computationally demanding for use in sparse autoencoders. In this work, we consider a family of comparatively efficient algorithms which we call \textbf{generalized matching pursuit (GMP) decoders}.

\subsection{Threshold Decoding and Top-$k$ Decoding}

To motivate one-step decoders, consider the problem of estimating a single coefficient $X_i$ from the model
\begin{equation}
	Y = X_i f_i + \overbrace{\sum_{j \neq i} X_j f_j}^{Z_i}. \label{eq:simple-model}
\end{equation}
(Throughout, our convention will be to write random variables with capital letters.) If we approximate the sum $Z_i$ as centered Gaussian noise with covariance $\Sigma,$ the inference problem for $X_i$ becomes tractable; specifically, if we define an inner product by $\langle v, w \rangle_\Sigma = \frac 1 2 v^T \Sigma^{-1} w,$ then the posterior on $X_i$ can be expressed in terms of the linear function
$$
	\lambda_i(Y) = \frac{\langle f_i, Y \rangle_\Sigma}{\lVert f \rVert_\Sigma^2}.
$$
In signal processing, this kind of optimal linear measurement is called a matched filter. In terms of the matched filter,
\begin{align} \label{eq:log_odds}
	 \ln \Pp(X_i = x|Y = y)  = -\frac {\rho_i} 2 \left(x - \lambda_i(y) \right)^2 + \ln \Pp(X_i = x) + C
\end{align}
where $\rho_i = (\Var \lambda_i(Z_i))^{-1}$ is called the ``signal-to-noise ratio.'' (See \Cref{appendix:matched} for a review.) When $F$ is a random dictionary of unit-norm codewords, it's natural to approximate the covariance $\Sigma$ by a factor of the identity, in which this case we have $\lambda_i(Y) = \langle f_i, Y \rangle / \lVert f_i \rVert = \langle f_i, Y \rangle.$

One simple decoding strategy would be to base our estimates $\hat X_i(Y) \approx X_i$ directly on the matched filters $\langle f_i, Y \rangle.$ Specifically, we may put $\hat X_i = 1$ if $\langle f_i, Y \rangle$ is larger than some threshold $\tau$ and $\hat X_i = 0$ otherwise. We refer to this as \textbf{threshold decoding} at level $\tau$ (Algorithm \ref{alg:threshold}). Substituting the prior
$$
\Pp(X_i = 0) = \frac{N - k}{N}, \quad \Pp(X_i = 1) = \frac k N
$$
into the posterior \eqref{eq:log_odds} for the Gaussian model shows that the maximum a posteriori estimate for $X_i$ corresponds to thresholding at level
\begin{equation}
\tau = \frac 1 2 - \rho_i^{-1} \ln \frac{\Pp(X_i = 1)}{\Pp(X_i = 0)} = \frac 1 2 - \rho_i^{-1} \ln \frac k {N - k}. \label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}

The inverse signal-to-noise ratios $\rho_i^{-1}$ can be estimated by computing the variance under a random dictionary $F.$ For both Rademacher and spherical dictionaries we have $\Var\, \langle F_i, F_j \rangle = 1/d$ for a pair of independent codewords, so
$$
\rho_i^{-1} = \Var \langle F_i, Y \rangle = \Var \langle F_i, F_1 + \dots + F_k \rangle
= \begin{cases}
(k - 1)/d & : i \in X \\
k/d & : \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$
Since we do not know in advance whether $i \in X,$ we take $\rho$ to be the expected value
\begin{align*}
\rho^{-1} & = \Pp(X_i = 1) \Var(\langle F_i, Z_i \rangle | X_i = 1)  + \Pp(X_i = 0) \Var(\langle F_i, Z_i \rangle | X_i = 1)  \\
    & = \frac{k(k - 1) + (N - k)k}{Nd} = \frac{k - k/N} d.
\end{align*}
Substituting this estimate into \eqref{eq:tau} gives
$$
\tau_{\text{MAP}} = \frac 1 2 - \frac {k - k/N} d \ln \frac{k}{N - k}.
$$
We refer to threshold decoding using this value of $\tau$ as MAP threshold decoding. We can simplify this expression in the regime $k/N = o(1)$ by noting that
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\text{MAP}} = \frac 1 2 - \frac k d \ln \frac k N + O\left( \frac k N \right). \label{eq:tau-map}
\end{equation}

Another very simple strategy is to first compute all the matched filters $F^T Y$ and find which are largest. We will call this top-$k$ decoding (Algorithm \ref{alg:top-k}). Top-$k$ has the disadvantage that $k$ must be known exactly in advance, whereas threshold decoding only requires an estimate of $k$ to adjust the threshold $\tau.$ However, should $k$ be known, it is clear that top-$k$ is strictly more reliable than threshold decoding for any $\tau.$

\begin{figure}[h]
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.48\textwidth}
\begin{algorithm}[H] \label{alg:threshold}
\KwIn{$y \in \R^d$, $F \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N}$, $\tau \in \R$}
\KwOut{$\hat{x} \in \{0,1\}^N$}
$\hat x \gets 0$ \;
$\hat x \gets 1 \text{ where } F^T y \ge \tau$ \;
\Return $\hat x$
\caption{Threshold decoding}
\end{algorithm}
\end{minipage}

\begin{algorithm}[H] \label{alg:top-k}
\KwIn{$y \in \mathbb{R}^d$, $F \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N}$, $k \in \N$}
\KwOut{$\hat{x} \in \{0,1\}^N$}
$\hat x \gets 0$ \;
$\hat x \gets 1 \text{ at $k$ largest values of } F^T y$ \;

\end{algorithm}

\end{figure}

Note that in some works \citep{elad_sparse_2010} top-$k$ is known as the thresholding algorithm. Finally, note that in practice the latent quantities $X_i$ to be estimated take values besides $0$ and $1.$ The two algorithms described here can be viewed as simplifications of one-step methods currently used by autoencoders, like soft thresholding ($\operatorname{ReLU}$ activation) and top-$k$. Sparse autoencoders using top-$k$ activations were first suggested by \citet{makhzani_k-sparse_2014}.

\subsection{GMP Decoding \label{sec:gmp}}

As discussed earlier, the field of compressive sensing provides various iterative algorithms for sparse recovery. For example, one classic starting point is to apply proximal gradient descent to the objective
$$
\frac {1}{2 d} \lVert F x - y \rVert^2 + \frac{\lambda}{d} \lVert x \rVert,.
$$
where the parameter $\lambda > 0$ can be understood as a Lagrange multiplier. This gives the iterative soft thresholding algorithm (ISTA) \citep{daubechies_iterative_2003}, and is the basis for methods like FISTA \citep{beck_fast_2009}. Unrolling ISTA and optimizing its parameters, as we would do for a deep neural network, is called learned ISTA (LISTA) \citep{gregor_learning_2010}. Another notable family of methods, which are not derived from a linear programming objective but instead from a message passing formulation, are based on the approximate message passing algorithm \citep{maleki_approximate_2010}. However, for sparse autoencoders, a training run using top-$k$ is already very computationally expensive. Therefore, it is interesting to study the properties of relatively cheap modifications to top-$k$.

We propose the following generalization of the top-$k$ decoder, called generalized matching pursuit (GMP).

\setlength{\algomargin}{1.5em}
\begin{algorithm}[h!]
\KwIn{Code $y \in \mathbb{R}^d$, dictionary $F \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N}$, sparsity $k$, steps $T$}
\KwOut{Latent $\hat{x} \in \{0,1\}^N$}
Choose any step sizes $k_1, \ldots, k_T$ so that $\sum_t k_t = k$ and $\max_t k_t$ is minimized \;
$\hat{x} \gets \mathbf{0}$ \;
\For{$t = 1, \dots, T$}{
    $\hat{x} \gets 1 \text{ at $k_t$ largest values of }
    F^T (y - F \hat x)$ \;
}
\Return $\hat{x}$
\caption{Generalized matching pursuit (GMP) with $T$ steps} \label{alg:gmp}
\end{algorithm}

This algorithm can be understood as a natural generalization of top-$k$, or as a simplification of generalized orthogonal matching pursuit \citep{wang_generalized_2012}. Since the most computationally expensive step of Algorithm \ref{alg:gmp} is to compute the product $F^T r$ where $r = y - F \hat x,$ while the vector $r$ can be efficiently updated from one iteration to the next, GMP with $T$ steps has roughly $T$ times the computational requirements of top-$k$. As we will see in our results in \Cref{sec:main}, $T = 3$ will be enough to significantly improve over the performance of top-$k.$
