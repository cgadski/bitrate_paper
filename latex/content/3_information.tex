\section{Information Theory Bounds \label{sec:information}}

To understand the minimum dimension $d$ required for a superposition code with parameters $(N, k)$ to be decodable by a given method, it will be helpful to use the point of view of information theory.

Let us first consider the case $k = 1$, meaning that the latent variable $X$ to be encoded is just a symbol drawn from an alphabet of size $N.$ What is the minimum dimension $d$ for which each value of $X$ can be represented by a vector $Y \in \R^d$?

When the vector $Y$ is stored exactly, the answer to our question simply depends on the number of values it can take. If the coefficients of $Y$ are $16$ bit floating point numbers, then each coefficient can store (nearly) $2^{16}$ distinct numbers, and overall we need only about $d_\text{min} \approx \frac 1 {16} \log_2 N$ dimensions. However, if the coordinates of $Y$ are subject to random interference, understanding its effective ``capacity'' to store information becomes a classical problem of information theory.

One common model is to consider that $Y$ is subject to additive white Gaussian noise. More specifically, we let $Z \in \R^d$ be a vector of independent Gaussians each of variance $P$ and consider the problem of recovering $X$ from $Y + Z.$ The following is then a typical (but remarkable) result of information theory.

\begin{proposition}[A Shannon limit] \label{prop:information}
    Let the random variable $X \in [N]$ be uniformly distributed and let $Z \in \R^d$ be independent Gaussian noise of variance $P.$ Suppose there exists a pair of maps $F \colon [N] \to \R^d$ and $G \colon \R^d \to [N]$ so that
	$$
		G(F(X) + Z) = X
	$$
	with probability at least $(1 - p),$ and suppose the variance of each coordinate of $F(X)$ is bounded by $1.$ Then
	$$
	d \geq 2 \frac{(1 - p) \ln N - \ln 2}{\ln \bigl( 1 + P^{-1} \bigr)}.
	$$
\end{proposition}

Asymptotically for large $N,$ this means that
$$
\frac d {H} \ge (1 + o(1)) \frac 2 {\ln (1 + P^{-1})}
$$
if there is any pair $(F, G)$ that transmits $X$ with probability greater than 0, where $H = \ln N$ is the entropy of $X.$ In other words, to transmit information reliably in the presence of noise of power $P,$ we need at least $2 / \ln(1 + P^{-1})$ dimensions per nat.

Conversely, it can be shown that this bound on the ratio $d/H$ is asymptotically tight. More specifically, for any $\epsilon > 0$ and for sufficiently large $N,$ it suffices that
$$
\frac d {H} \ge (1 + \epsilon) \frac 2 {\ln (1 + P^{-1})}
$$
for there to exist some pair $(F, G)$ satisfying the conditions of \Cref{prop:information} with $p$ arbitrarily close to 0. The same conclusions hold more generally under different models for noisy transmission, substituting $P^{-1}$ in general for a ``signal-to-noise ratio'' (SNR). For a reference on these topics, see e.g. Chapter 9 of \citet{cover_elements_2006}.

In the remainder of this work, we will apply the information-theoretic point of view to our toy superposition coding problem. Specifically, we will measure the performance of a given decoding method in terms of the asymptotically attainable inverse ``bitrate'' $d / H,$ where $d$ is the dimension of our superposition code and $H = \ln \binom N k$ is the entropy of the $k$-element subset to be transmitted.

Ideally, it would be possible to decode a superposition code so long as $d/H$ is larger than some fixed lower bound. Informally, this means that the decoder would be able to read a non-vanishing amount of information from each dimension of the code. In this scenario, we will say that a decoder is ``information-efficient." Since $\ln \binom N k = k \ln (N/k) + O(k) \le k \ln (e N/k)$ (see \Cref{appendix:binomial}) information-efficiency means having an asymptotically sufficient condition of the form
$$
d \ge C k \ln (eN/k)
$$
for some constant $C.$ In fact, this kind of condition is proven in compressive sensing for certain sparse reconstruction methods based on linear programming; see Inequality (1.2) in \citet{donoho_counting_2009}. In the remainder of this work, we will study the constants $C$ required by the three efficient decoding methods described above.

In principle, the constant $C$ could be as small as $\log(S)^{-1},$ where $S$ is the number of states representable by each coordinate of the code $Y.$ However, we will see that the actual value of $C$ tends to be signifiantly larger. In fact, for $k > 2,$ all three methods we study turn out to require more than \textit{one dimension per bit}. Before proceeding to our main results, which are the subject of the next section, let us see how a requirement of one bit per dimension can be explained (very informally) using a Shannon limit.

Let us divide the vector $X \in \R^N$ into two parts $(X^0, X^1)$ each of length $N/2$ and $k/2$ non-zero entries, and likewise divide the dictionary $F$ into two dictionaries $(F^0, F^1)$ so that $Y = F^0 X^0 + F^1 X^1.$ Now, crucially, suppose we are using a decoder whose estimate for $X^0$ is independent of $F^1,$ and vice versa. If our decoder is reliable for a randomly constructed dictionary, then it follows that the restriction of our decoder to $X^0$ is reliable under a noisy channel with signal-to-noise ratio of 1. Information theory tells us the inverse capacity of such a channel is $2 / \ln(1 + 1) = 2 / \ln 2,$ and so
$$
d \ge (1 + o(1)) \frac{2}{\ln 2} \ln \binom{N/2}{k/2}.
$$
Since overall the information to be transmitted is $H = 2 \ln \binom{N/2}{k/2},$ we conclude
$$
\frac d H \ge (1 + o(1)) \frac{1}{\ln 2}.
$$
Very roughly, this shows that if a decoder can separated into two pieces that almost do not communicate, then it cannot read more than $1$ bit per dimension from a superposition code. However, this kind of decoupling is an important characteristic of many efficient algorithms, and should intuitively hold in an approximate sense for our three decoding methods.

On the other hand, if our estimate $\hat X_i$ for each coordinate $X_i$ is independent from \textit{all} the codewords $\{F_j\}_{i \neq j}$---as is the case in threshold decoding---then each estimate $\hat X_i$ is reliable under noise with a signal-to-noise ratio of nearly $k.$ For a channel with SNR of $k$ the Shannon limit tells us that
$$
\frac d H \ge (1 + o(1)) \frac{2}{\ln(1 + k^{-1})} \approx 2 (1 + o(1)) k
$$
for large $k,$ and so we expect to need around $2 k \ln N$ dimensions.
